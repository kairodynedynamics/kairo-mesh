Disaster communication · Made in Türkiye

When the network is down, the call for help still reaches the authority.

Kairo Mesh lets phones carry each other's encrypted messages over Bluetooth, with no internet and no cell towers. A call for help typed under rubble travels from phone to phone, and the first phone that reaches the internet hands it to the emergency authority in the background.

Status

Research prototype, working on Android, iOS and a reference server. Simulation results and 79 automated tests are public. Field trials with real phones and an independent security audit are the next steps.

How it works

  1. Tap, or type

    The emergency screen needs no typing: choose the situation (under rubble, injured, fire), the number of people and who is with you. GPS position is added; GPS works without a network. Free text is turned into a structured record on the phone.

  2. Sealed to the authority

    The call is sealed with the authority's public key using the Noise X pattern (Noise_X_25519_ChaChaPoly_SHA256), checked byte for byte against a published test vector on every platform. Carriers see neither content nor location nor sender.

  3. Carried by any phone

    Every phone with the app carries emergency calls without its owner doing anything. Emergency calls always go first and move toward phones that are more often online.

  4. Delivered and receipted

    The first phone with internet uploads the call to the authority's server. The authority's receipt, which only it can produce, travels back through the mesh: the caller sees "the authority has your call".

Built for public authorities

  • The authority generates its keys on its own machine; the private key never leaves it.
  • The server runs in-country, on the authority's infrastructure. A dispatcher dashboard lists calls by urgency.
  • Signed announcements (assembly points, water distribution, unsafe buildings) flow back to the public through the same mesh.
  • Forged emergency calls are bounded by proof of work and a per-phone quota.
  • The core is open source (AGPL-3.0) for audit and trust; commercial and institutional licences are available.
